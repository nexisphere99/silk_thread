## Passage: R2_Opening

I have been staring at this binder for forty minutes.

Three binders, actually. Color-coded. The Henderson account sits in front of me in triplicate because I am the kind of man who believes preparation is a personality. The blue binder holds the primary deck. The red one holds contingency slides for six different objection scenarios I mapped out over the weekend while Lena watched something on Netflix that made her laugh in a way I haven't caused in over a year. The green binder holds financials. Backup financials. Financials for the financials.

It is 5:22 AM on a Tuesday.

My alarm is set for 5:45 but I have been awake since 4:30, lying next to Lena in the dark, running the Henderson presentation in my head like a film reel stuck on loop. Every slide. Every transition. Every anticipated question and its rehearsed, polished answer. I mouthed the words silently while she slept with her back to me, auburn hair across the pillow, phone face-down on her nightstand like a secret she'd tucked away for the night.

I get up without disturbing her. This used to require care. Now it requires nothing. She sleeps through my mornings the way you sleep through traffic noise after living on a busy street long enough. I am ambient. Background. The hum of a refrigerator she has stopped hearing.

Shower. Seven minutes. Same temperature I have used since we moved in. I shave jawline first because my father shaved jawline first and some inheritances you don't choose, they just live inside your muscle memory like dormant code. The shirt is already on the hanger. Pale blue today, conservative. The Henderson people are old-money energy, the kind of clients who judge your cufflinks and never mention it. My shoes are by the door. Mirror shine. I catch my reflection in the bathroom glass and see a man who looks like he has it together. Tie straight, collar pressed, jaw clean.

I look exactly like someone who is about to lose everything.

I just don't know it yet.

## Passage: R2_Kitchenette

The Meridian lobby smells like lilies and fresh carpet. The security guard nods. The elevator doors close around me like a glass coffin and I ride forty floors watching the number climb and trying to slow my heart rate through controlled breathing, which is a technique I read about in a men's health article that also recommended cold showers and journaling. I do none of these things consistently. I just breathe in the elevator and pretend it helps.

Floor 39. My floor. Mid-level. Interior-facing. No skyline view. I know where my office sits in the hierarchy the way an animal knows its position in the pack. Not the alpha. Not the runt. The one who survives by not drawing attention.

I am early. The floor is almost empty. Fluorescent lights hum their cold song over rows of gray desks and ergonomic chairs that cost more than my first car. My office is a glass-walled box with a door that doesn't lock. I set the three binders on my desk in order. Blue, red, green. I open my laptop. I check my email. I check it again. I rehearse the opening line of my presentation one more time. I say it out loud to the empty room. It sounds fine. It sounds competent. That should be enough. That has always been enough.

Harper Quinn materializes in the kitchenette doorway while I am pouring coffee with hands that are not shaking but are considering it.

"You okay?" She leans against the doorframe. Twenty-six years old, fitted blazer, hair pulled back, eyes that miss nothing. Harper studies people the way ornithologists study migration patterns. Silently. Over long periods. Taking notes. "You look like you're about to present to God."

"Henderson. Ten o'clock."

"I know. I revised the deck for you yesterday." She says this without accusation. She revised it because she caught three inconsistencies in my data tables that I missed because I was so busy preparing that I forgot to actually check my work. This is the story of my career compressed into a single sentence.

"You've got this," she says. Genuine. Warm. The kind of encouragement that should land like a life raft but instead slides past me because I am already drowning in the part of my brain that rehearses catastrophe.

"Thanks, Harper."

She pours her own coffee. Black, no sugar. She drinks it like medicine. She watches me over the rim. I can feel the observation even after I turn back to my binder. Some people look at you. Harper *reads* you.

I take my coffee back to my office and close the glass door which provides the illusion of privacy while actually providing none, which is, now that I think about it, a reasonable metaphor for my entire professional existence.

## Passage: R2_Henderson_Meeting

Conference Room A, Floor 41. The executive floor. Everything up here is heavier. The carpet. The silence. The consequences.

The Henderson team arrives at 9:58. Four of them. Margaret Walsh leads. She is the CFO and she smiles like she's auditing your soul and finding the balance short. Gray suit, reading glasses she doesn't need but wears because they make people underestimate her for the three seconds before she asks a question that makes them sweat through their shirts.

Two junior analysts flank her. They carry tablets and say nothing. The fourth is their legal counsel, a man named Braddock who sits with his arms crossed for the entire meeting like a bouncer at the door of a club I am about to be thrown out of.

I begin the presentation. I am polished. I am prepared. For ten minutes I am the version of myself that my father would approve of. Controlled. Certain. Direct.

Then Margaret asks about Q3 projections under a regulatory scenario I didn't model.

The question is reasonable. It is the kind of question a competent CFO asks when evaluating strategic risk. It is the question I should have anticipated. It is sitting right there in the gap between my blue binder and my red binder, in the space where a fourth binder should have existed if I had been thorough instead of merely busy.

I pivot. This is the wrong word for what I do. Pivoting implies grace, a dancer changing direction. What I do is lunge. I push a growth-oriented strategy because growth is what I prepared, growth is what I know, growth is the hammer I brought to a meeting that turns out to need a scalpel.

Margaret wants risk mitigation. She says so with her eyes before she says so with her mouth. The room temperature drops. Her smile goes from auditing to foreclosing. I keep talking. I am saying the right words in the wrong order for the wrong audience at the wrong time and I know it and I cannot stop because stopping would mean admitting I don't have the answer and Daniel Ashford always has the answer, that is the deal, that is the contract I signed with the universe when I polished my shoes and ironed my shirts and married a good woman and did everything right.

I double down.

Margaret's face closes like a door. Not slammed. Clicked shut. Quietly, deliberately, with the finality of a lock turning.

The meeting ends fifteen minutes early. She shakes my hand. Her grip is firm and brief and empty of everything that was in it an hour ago.

"We'll be in touch."

We both know what that means. "We'll be in touch" is corporate for "don't call us." It is the business equivalent of a doctor saying "we'll make you comfortable." Terminal. Irrevocable. Kind only in its brevity.

The Henderson team files out. Braddock uncrosses his arms for the first time. The junior analysts don't look at me. Margaret walks through the door and takes twelve million dollars with her.

I stand alone in Conference Room A. The projector is still on. My slide deck glows on the screen, frozen on a bar chart showing projected growth that will never happen now. The bars point upward. Optimistic. Idiotic. The chart of a man who built three binders and forgot to build the one that mattered.

## Passage: R2_Greg_Office

Greg Tanaka is waiting.

Of course he is. Greg Tanaka is my direct superior and he has been watching the Henderson account like a hawk watches a mouse it knows it's going to eat. He just didn't expect to be eating his own team member instead of the revenue.

His office door is already open. This is worse than if it were closed. A closed door means a conversation. An open door means an execution.

"Sit."

I sit.

Greg's office is neat the way an operating room is neat. Everything has a purpose. Nothing is decorative. There is a photo of his family on the desk, turned at an angle that lets visitors see it without letting Greg get distracted by sentiment during working hours. He is efficient even in his affections.

"Henderson was a twelve-million-dollar relationship, Ashford." He says my name like punctuation. Like a period at the end of a sentence I didn't write. "Was."

I open my mouth. He holds up one hand.

"Don't. I watched the recording. You pushed growth on a risk-averse CFO who told you three times what she wanted and you ignored her three times because you only prepared one playbook."

He is right. He is precisely, surgically, lethally right.

"I'm putting in a call to Margaret this afternoon. If I can walk it back, I will. But you should know that I am also drafting the paperwork."

"What paperwork?"

He looks at me. The look says: you already know what paperwork.

Termination paperwork. Not a threat. A logistical reality. Greg Tanaka doesn't threaten. He processes. He is already composing the email in his head, the one that begins with "It is with regret" and ends with "effective immediately." I can see the cursor blinking behind his eyes.

"I'll fix this," I say.

"No, Daniel. You won't. Because you've been on thin ice since the Beaumont near-miss last year and this isn't thin ice anymore. This is open water."

I stand. My legs work fine. My hands are steady. My face is composed. Everything about my exterior is functioning exactly as designed while the interior experiences something between freefall and cardiac arrest.

"I'll let you know by end of week," Greg says.

I nod. Walk out. His door is still open behind me. I don't close it. Closing it would be an act of agency and I don't have any of that left.

## Passage: R2_Elevator_Silence

The elevator takes forty floors to reach the lobby. I know this because I count them. Not the numbers on the display. The seconds. The small mechanical sounds. The hum of cables doing their honest work, transporting the weight of a man whose career is a flatline.

My reflection stares back from the polished steel doors. Tie still straight. Shirt still pressed. Shoes still holding their Sunday shine. I look like a man who went to a meeting and came back. I look like every other person in this elevator on every other day. The collapse is entirely internal, which means it is entirely invisible, which means I will walk through the lobby and the security guard will nod and I will nod back and the lilies will smell exactly the same as they did this morning and nothing in the physical world will register that Daniel Ashford just cratered the biggest account on his portfolio and is now actively being processed for removal like a tumor that the body has decided to excise.

I do not cry. Daniel Ashford does not cry. My father did not cry. My father's father, from what I was told, also did not cry, and they both died early of heart-related complications which I am certain is unrelated.

I breathe. Carefully. The controlled breathing from the men's health article. In through the nose, hold, out through the mouth. The elevator descends. Thirty-two. Thirty-one. Thirty. I watch the numbers and I breathe and I do not cry and I do not scream and I do not punch the polished steel because that would leave a mark and Daniel Ashford does not leave marks. Daniel Ashford is a man who passes through rooms like clean water through a filter.

No residue. No trace. Nothing that says I was here.

The lobby doors open. The security guard nods. I nod back.

## Passage: R2_Afternoon_Fog

I don't leave the building. That would be an admission. Instead I go back to my desk and sit in front of my laptop and stare at the Henderson binders, which are still arranged in their neat little row, blue red green, a color-coded monument to inadequacy. I should throw them away. I leave them there.

The afternoon passes in a fog. I answer emails. I attend a team check-in and contribute nothing. I nod when people speak. I drink three cups of coffee that taste like hot brown water. At 2:15 I go to the men's room and stand in front of the mirror and look at my face and try to identify the exact moment I became the kind of man who loses twelve-million-dollar accounts.

It's not a moment. That's the problem. It's a gradient. A slow downward slope so gentle you don't notice you're descending until you look up and the summit is gone. I wasn't sharp enough. I wasn't bold enough. I prepared more than anyone and understood less than most. I built buffers and binders and backup plans and none of it mattered because the core product, the man himself, was average. And average, in a building like this, is a death sentence delivered on a long enough timeline.

Harper stops by my desk at 4:30.

"How'd Henderson go?"

"Fine."

She stands there. Does not leave. Harper does not accept "fine" from anyone, least of all from a man whose face is doing whatever mine is doing right now.

"Fine like it went well, or fine like you don't want to talk about it?"

"The second one."

She nods. Doesn't push. But she sets a granola bar on my desk before she walks away, the kind with chocolate chips that she keeps in her drawer for long days, and the small kindness almost breaks the seal on something I am holding shut with every muscle in my chest.

I eat the granola bar. It tastes like chocolate and pity.

## Passage: R2_Dinner_Silence

Lena made stir-fry. The good kind, with the sesame oil and the snap peas she gets from the farmer's market on Saturdays. She used to only make this when she was in a good mood. I don't know what mood she's in tonight. I don't know what mood she's been in for weeks. We coexist in the apartment the way books coexist on a shelf. Side by side. Spines touching. Never opening each other.

"How was your day?"

"Long."

She nods. Picks up her fork. We eat. The television is on because the television is always on because silence between married people requires management and we have outsourced ours to a flat screen that neither of us is watching.

The stir-fry is good. I eat it mechanically. Each bite is chewed and swallowed and registered by the biological machinery of my body while the psychological machinery replays the Henderson meeting on an infinite loop. Margaret's face closing. Greg's paperwork. The elevator numbers descending. The security guard's nod.

"You're quiet tonight," Lena says.

"Just tired."

She looks at me. Really looks, for the first time in what might be days. Her eyes are hazel. I used to write about her eyes. Early in our relationship, before the wedding, I wrote her a letter that described them as the color of October. I was better at things then. Or I tried harder. I'm not sure those are different statements.

"You sure?"

"Yeah. Just. Big presentation today. Drained me."

She accepts this. Not because it's convincing but because probing further would require an energy investment in our marriage that neither of us has budgeted for this quarter. We are running on emotional reserves and the reserves are dry. We both know it. Neither of us has called the audit.

We finish dinner. She washes the dishes. I dry. We perform the choreography with the precision of dancers who have rehearsed until the steps are muscle memory and the music is something neither of us can hear anymore.

"I'm going to bed early," she says. "Headache."

"Okay."

She pauses at the bedroom door. Turns.

"You sure you're okay, Daniel?"

The way she says my name. Soft. Genuine. For one second she is the woman I married, the one who would sit on the bathroom floor with me when I was anxious and hold my hand and say nothing, which was always the right amount to say. For one second the distance collapses and I could tell her everything. The meeting. Margaret. Greg. The paperwork. The twelve million dollars and the open water.

I could let her in. She's standing right there, hand on the doorframe, waiting. She wants to connect. I can see it in the tilt of her head, the way her fingers curl against the wood. She is offering me a rope and all I have to do is grab it.

"I'm fine. Get some rest."

She lingers. One beat. Two. The rope retracts.

"Okay. Night."

The bedroom door closes. Softly, like she's trying not to disturb something fragile. The click of the latch is the loudest sound in the apartment.

## Passage: R2_Whiskey_Alone

I pour whiskey. Bourbon. Maker's Mark, the bottle we got as a housewarming gift from Lena's cousin two years ago and have been slowly working through, mostly me, mostly on nights like this. The glass is heavy in my hand. The first sip burns and then blooms and then settles into a warmth that has nothing to do with comfort and everything to do with chemical alteration of consciousness, which is a clinical way of saying I am drinking alone on a Tuesday night because I destroyed my career today and told my wife I was fine.

I sit on the couch. The apartment is dark except for the kitchen light Lena left on, which casts a yellow rectangle across the hardwood floor like a spotlight on an empty stage. I drink. I replay.

The meeting. Take one. I open with the growth strategy. Margaret asks about regulatory risk. I pivot. I model the risk on the fly, pulling numbers from the contingency binder. Margaret nods. She's engaged. I hold the room. The meeting runs to completion. Henderson stays. Twelve million secured.

Fantasy. Cut.

The meeting. Take two. The real one. I open with the growth strategy. Margaret asks about regulatory risk. I do not pivot. I barrel forward like a man trying to drive through a wall because he can't find the door. I lose the room. I lose the account. I lose everything.

I drink more whiskey. The glass empties. I pour another.

My father would tell me to get up early tomorrow. Work harder. Push through. "Men don't sit in the dark feeling sorry for themselves, Daniel. Men act." My father was a machine shaped like a man and I have spent my entire adult life trying to pass his inspections from beyond the grave.

I have been passing. Barely. The polished shoes and ironed shirts and organized closets are all his. The controlled breathing and the dry eyes and the "I'm fine" are all his. I am a monument to his blueprint, accurate in every surface detail and hollow at the center, a building with no foundation just a shiny exterior that everyone assumes means the structure is sound.

I drink until the bottle is lighter and the meeting is fuzzier and the bedroom door's click has faded to an echo. Then I sit in the dark and I stare at the wall and I feel the career I built with ten years of Sunday night ironing dissolve around me like sugar in water.

Gone. All of it. Quiet enough that nobody heard it happen.

## Passage: R2_Late_Night_Email

11:47 PM.

I am on my third whiskey and my seventh replay of the meeting when my phone buzzes on the coffee table. The screen lights up and throws the notification at the ceiling like a tiny searchlight.

Email. The sender line reads: Victoria Cross.

Victoria Cross. I know the name the way you know the name of a weather system that hasn't hit your coast yet. Senior VP. New vertical lead. The woman who reorganized three divisions in her first quarter and didn't apologize for any of it. People talk about her in the hallways with the careful neutrality they usually reserve for discussing natural disasters and upper management, which are, in many ways, the same thing.

I have never interacted with her directly. She exists two floors above me in every sense. I've seen her in all-hands meetings, standing at the front of the room with the posture of someone who has never been uncertain about anything. Dark hair, sharp jaw, heels that sound like a metronome on the marble. People move out of her way without being asked, the way fish part for something larger moving through the water.

The email has no subject line beyond my name.

I open it.

*"My office. 7 AM. Don't be late."*

Nine words. No greeting. No context. No pleasantries. No sign-off.

I read it six times. The whiskey in my stomach turns to acid. The warmth drains from the glass in my hand. My thumb hovers over the screen like it's trying to extract additional information from the pixels through proximity.

7 AM. Victoria Cross's office. Floor 42. The top. The summit I have spent my career being too afraid to approach.

She knows. Of course she knows. Henderson was twelve million and twelve million doesn't vanish without the Senior VP of the new vertical noticing. Greg probably called her. Or she saw the report. Or she has some system I don't know about, some wire tapped into the building's nervous system that alerts her when a mid-level account director drives a client relationship into the ground like a plane with no landing gear.

7 AM. She wants to fire me herself. Or she wants to watch while someone else does. Or she wants something I can't imagine yet because my imagination is a small room with low ceilings and Victoria Cross operates from somewhere that doesn't appear on any floor plan I've been given.

I finish the whiskey. I set the glass in the sink. I brush my teeth. I get into bed next to Lena, who is asleep, her breathing even, her body turned toward the window, her phone face-down on the nightstand.

I lie there. The ceiling is white. The sheets are cool. My wife breathes beside me in a rhythm that used to be comforting and now is just present. The email glows in my memory like a hot coal.

*"Don't be late."*

I set my alarm for 5:30. Fifteen minutes earlier than usual. Building a buffer for whatever is coming. Because that's what Daniel Ashford does. He prepares. He buffers. He organizes by color and polishes to a shine and shows up early and none of it has ever saved him from anything.

Sleep doesn't come for a long time. When it does, I dream about elevators and falling numbers and Margaret Walsh's face clicking shut.

## Passage: R2_Martin_Crane_Hallway

Martin Crane catches me in the corridor between the kitchenette and my office. This is not an accident. Martin Crane does not have accidental encounters. He occupies space with intention, a 55-year-old continent of tailored wool and aftershave that smells like old money and diesel.

"Ashford." The handshake is automatic. My hand disappears into his like a letter into a mailbox. "Heard Henderson went sideways."

Word travels fast. Word travels at the speed of failure. Success is a rumor. Failure is breaking news.

"It's being handled."

"Is it." This is not a question. Martin Crane does not ask questions. He makes statements and inflects them upward to give you the illusion of agency.

He studies me. Brown eyes behind glasses that cost more than my monthly car payment. Martin is the old guard. He built Meridian's client base through handshakes and golf games and the unshakeable certainty of a man who has never once doubted his place in any room. He is everything my father wanted me to be. Tall, broad, certain, occupying space like it owes him rent.

"You need to be careful, Daniel. This firm remembers. People forgive mistakes. Institutions don't."

He claps my shoulder. The same half-approval, half-ownership gesture from every other time. But today it feels like he's checking if I'm still standing. Or checking if I'm worth catching.

"Henderson can be recovered. If someone with enough weight picks up the phone."

"Someone like who?"

He smiles. The kind of smile that contains information but doesn't release it.

"Just keep your head down. And for God's sake, stop looking like the world ended. Even when it did."

He walks away. His shoes make no sound on the carpet. Men like Martin Crane have been absorbing the impact of their own footsteps for so long the floor has given up registering them.

## Passage: R2_Choice_Lena

[CHOICE PASSAGE - Triggers after Scene 4: Home Dinner]

The bedroom door is open. Lena stands there in her sleep shirt, the oversized one from a 5K charity run she did three years ago, and she looks at me the way you look at furniture you've been meaning to rearrange but keep putting off.

"You sure you're okay?"

This is the moment. The fulcrum. Everything pivots here.

### Choice A: Tell Lena about the fuckup

I sit on the edge of the bed. The mattress dips under my weight.

"I lost Henderson today."

She turns. Full attention. This is already more than I've given her in weeks and we both feel the gravity of it.

"What happened?"

"I misread the room. Pushed the wrong strategy. The CFO walked. Greg's drafting termination paperwork."

Saying it out loud is like pulling a splinter. The pain comes after. Lena sits next to me. Close. Her hand finds my knee and rests there like a small warm anchor.

"Oh, Daniel."

She is sympathetic. Genuinely. But I watch her face and I see the calculus happening behind the compassion. She is thinking about the mortgage. The car payments. The life we built on two incomes and Sunday routines. She is worried. Not for me. For us. For the structure.

I came to her for comfort and what I see is concern. Practical concern. The kind that calculates before it consoles.

"We'll figure it out," she says. And she means it. But the "we" sounds different now. It sounds like a budget meeting.

I feel something shift inside me. Something small and significant. The seed of a realization that will grow into something I don't have a name for yet: the awareness that being rescued might feel better than rescuing. That someone else holding the wheel might be exactly what I need.

She squeezes my knee. Kisses my cheek. Turns off her lamp.

I lie beside her in the dark, and for the first time in my marriage, the vulnerability feels like a door opening instead of a wall falling down.

**[Stat Impact: Marriage Bond +3, Submission +2]**

### Choice B: Keep it to himself

"I'm fine. Just tired."

She watches me. Weighing. Deciding whether to push. I can see the moment she decides not to. The small surrender in her shoulders.

"Okay. Night."

She turns. I turn. We lie in the same bed facing opposite walls and the three feet between our spines might as well be the Atlantic. She offered a rope. I let it drop. She won't offer it again tonight. Maybe not tomorrow either.

I carry the Henderson disaster alone. I carry it through the whiskey and the dark apartment and the late-night email from Victoria Cross. It sits in my chest like a stone and I hold it there because holding things alone is what I do. It is my only consistent skill. The one thing I never fail at.

The distance widens. I can hear it happening. Not a sound, exactly. More like the absence of a sound that used to be there. A frequency dropping out.

## Passage: R2_End_Reflection

The apartment is dark. The whiskey is doing its slow chemical work on my nervous system, softening the edges but not erasing them. Nothing erases them.

Tomorrow at 7 AM I will sit in Victoria Cross's office and she will say whatever she is going to say and my career will either end or it won't and either way the man who walks out of that meeting will be different from the man who walks in.

I don't know this yet. I think I do. I think I can see the shape of what's coming. But I am looking at the surface and the surface is all I've ever known how to read. Underneath, in the dark water where the real currents move, something is already shifting. Something I have no name for. Something that has been waiting.

I finish the last of the whiskey. Rinse the glass. Place it in the dish rack, exactly centered, because I am a man who centers his glassware and polishes his shoes and loses twelve-million-dollar accounts and tells his wife he's fine.

In the bedroom, Lena breathes.

On my phone, Victoria Cross's email glows.

I set the alarm. 5:30. Fifteen minutes earlier than usual.

I close my eyes and fall into something that is not quite sleep and not quite waking but some liminal in-between where the Henderson meeting plays on loop and Margaret Walsh's smile clicks shut and Greg Tanaka drafts paperwork and Martin Crane claps my shoulder and Harper's sticky note says *check pg 14* and I didn't check and I should have checked and it's too late now.

It's too late for a lot of things.

It's early for everything else.

## Passage: R2_Dream_Sequence

### Harper Quinn - Extended Office Interaction

[Triggers if player visits Harper's desk during the afternoon]

Harper is back from lunch. She has a salad in a clear container and she eats it while reading a report, which is the most Harper Quinn thing I have ever witnessed. Multitasking her nutrition.

"Hey," I say.

She looks up. Pauses with a forkful of arugula suspended between the container and her mouth. "Hey yourself. You look like you swallowed a coat hanger."

"Henderson tanked."

"How?"

"Because Greg's door has been closed all afternoon and you've been walking through the office like a ghost who forgot which room he died in." She takes a bite of salad. Chews. "Also Tanaka looked like he wanted to throw his laptop off the building, which is a very specific expression I've only seen twice."

"I messed up, Harper. I pushed growth when she wanted risk mitigation and I didn't have the regulatory model ready and I doubled down instead of pulling back."

"I know."

"You left a note on your monitor. Page 14."

She stops chewing. "You saw that?"

"Just now. After the meeting."

She swallows. Looks at her salad. Looks at me. In her eyes I see something complicated. The frustration of a woman who saw the train coming and waved and wasn't seen. The tenderness of a colleague who has been quietly catching my mistakes for months without keeping score. And underneath both, something else entirely. A warmth that has nothing to do with mentorship.

"I should have been louder about it," she says.

"No. I should have listened."

We sit in silence for a moment. The office hums around us. Fluorescent lights. Keyboard clicks. The world continuing without the Henderson account, as worlds do.

"Whatever happens with this," she says, "I've got your back on the portfolio review. Just so you know."

"Why?"

She shrugs. "Because you'd do it for me. And because someone should." She picks up her fork. "Now go away. You're making my lunch depressing."

I stand. At her desk, among the orbiting notebooks and the cat-shaped cactus and the dog-eared paperback, I see another sticky note, half-hidden under a file folder. In Harper's sharp handwriting: *"DA - tell him about the gap??"*

Two question marks. She debated it. She chose the note on her monitor instead of the conversation. She was trying to help without overstepping. She was navigating the politics of being a 26-year-old woman correcting a 31-year-old man's work in a firm that runs on hierarchy and ego.

I walk back to my desk with the knowledge that Harper Quinn saw me failing in slow motion and tried to catch me without being seen trying. And the knowledge that I was so busy preparing that I never once looked in her direction.

### Martin Crane - Extended Office Side Quest

[Triggers if player visits Martin's office before the end of the work day]

Martin's office is on 41. I shouldn't be up here. My floor is 39 and the two-floor difference is a border crossing, a class distinction written in carpet and square footage.

His door is open. Martin Crane's door is always open because Martin Crane has nothing to hide and wants you to know it. Or wants you to think it. These might be different things.

"Ashford. Come in."

His office has a skyline view. The city stretches to the horizon and the late afternoon sun turns the glass towers into columns of gold. Martin sits behind a desk made of wood that predates the building. There are awards on the wall. Framed articles. A photo of him shaking hands with a senator.

"Sit. Drink?"

It is 4 PM on a Tuesday and he is offering me liquor from a crystal decanter that lives on his credenza. This is either a mentorship gesture or a wake. I accept because declining a drink from Martin Crane is something my father would never do.

Scotch. Single malt. It burns differently than the bourbon at home. Cleaner. More expensive.

"I've been at this firm twenty-three years," he says. He doesn't look at me when he speaks. He looks at the skyline. "Came in as an associate. Younger than your girl Quinn. Built this client base from a Rolodex and a firm handshake."

He turns the glass in his hand. The scotch catches the light.

"You know what kills a career here, Daniel? Not failure. Failure is Tuesday. What kills a career is panic. The man who loses a client and starts sweating, starts scrambling, starts looking like he's drowning. That man is finished. Not because of the loss. Because of the optics."

He looks at me now. Full attention. Martin Crane's full attention is like standing in a searchlight.

"You lost Henderson. Fine. It's gone. Stop carrying the body. Put it down. Walk forward."

"Greg is drafting paperwork."

"Greg drafts a lot of things. Greg drafted my termination paperwork in 2009 when I lost the Comstock account. You know what happened?"

"What?"

"Someone with authority picked up the phone and told Greg to put the paperwork in the shredder." He finishes his scotch. "The question isn't whether someone will make that call for you. The question is whether you're worth the call."

He pours another measure. Doesn't offer me a second. One drink is mentorship. Two drinks is indulgence.

"Go home, Ashford. Sleep. Come back tomorrow looking like a man who still belongs here."

I stand. My glass is empty.

"Sir?"

"For God's sake. Stop ironing your shirts so precisely. It makes you look like you're trying."

He turns back to the skyline. I'm dismissed. But not unkindly. Martin Crane operates on the same emotional bandwidth as a lighthouse. Steady. Bright. Visible from a great distance. Never warm.
