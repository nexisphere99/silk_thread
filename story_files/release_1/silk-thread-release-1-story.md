### PASSAGE: opening_alarm

The alarm goes off at 5:45 AM. Fifteen minutes early, the way it always does, the way I set it three years ago when I decided that being early was the same thing as being in control. It isn't. But the alarm doesn't know that and neither do I, most mornings.

I lie there for eleven seconds. I know it's eleven because I count them. Some men pray. Some men reach for their wives. I count to eleven and then I swing my legs off the mattress and plant my feet on the hardwood and that's it, that's the beginning, every single weekday for the last four years.

Lena's hair is fanned across her pillow like something from a shampoo commercial, auburn catching the thin line of streetlight that leaks through the blinds. She used to get up with me. We'd make coffee together, bump hips at the kitchen counter, her in my old college t-shirt, me in boxers, and the apartment would smell like dark roast and sleep-warm skin. That was year one. Maybe early year two. Now she sleeps until I leave, and I kiss her forehead on the way out, and she doesn't stir, and neither of us mentions the change because naming it would make it real.

I kiss her forehead. She doesn't stir.

The apartment is quiet in the way that expensive mid-rise apartments are quiet. Insulated. Sealed. The furniture is from West Elm and CB2 and one piece from Restoration Hardware that cost more than it should have because Lena's mother was visiting and I wanted the living room to say something about me. I'm not sure what it says. Probably "adequate." Probably "trying."

### PASSAGE: morning_routine

The shower takes exactly seven minutes. I've timed it. Water at 102 degrees because I read somewhere that's the optimal temperature for circulation without damaging your skin, and I filed that fact away the same way I file everything: neatly, permanently, uselessly.

Shaving. Jawline first, then cheeks, then upper lip, then the neck in careful downward strokes. My father taught me to shave when I was fourteen. He stood behind me in the bathroom mirror, his hand over mine on the razor, guiding the angle. "A man's face is his handshake with the world, Danny." He said things like that. Declarations that sounded carved from granite but meant nothing when you held them up to light. He was career military. He could field-strip a rifle but not a conversation. He could command a room but not a marriage. My mother left when I was nine and he blamed softness. Everything after that was about making me hard enough to keep.

I pick the shirt I selected last night. Light blue, Egyptian cotton, pressed on Sunday along with four others while football played in the background. I don't care about football. I care about the appearance of caring about football, which is its own exhausting sport.

Tie. Windsor knot, the only one my father showed me, the only one I've ever tied. Shoes by the door, polished to a mirror sheen. I can see my own face in the toe cap, distorted, smaller than it should be.

I grab my briefcase. Keys. Phone. Wallet. The same four objects in the same four pockets every morning, a ritual so ingrained it's closer to prayer than habit.

At the door, I stop. Look back at the hallway leading to our bedroom where Lena is still sleeping. The distance between the front door and that bedroom is twenty-three feet. It feels longer every month.

I leave.

### PASSAGE: commute

The drive to Meridian takes twenty-two minutes without traffic, thirty-one with. Today it's twenty-eight. I listen to a podcast about market disruption that I'll reference in a meeting to sound current and then immediately forget. The host has the kind of voice that suggests he's never lost sleep over anything, and I envy that, the luxury of certainty.

Downtown materializes through the windshield like a promise I'm not sure I believe anymore. Glass towers, steel bones, the whole city reaching upward because that's what cities do. Meridian Consulting occupies floors 38 through 42 of one of these towers. I work on 39. Interior-facing. No skyline view. I am aware of this hierarchy the way a dog is aware of the fence around its yard. Not with resentment, exactly. More like permanent, low-grade understanding of where the boundary is.

The parking garage is four levels underground. I park in the same spot every day, not because it's assigned but because I'm the kind of person who parks in the same spot every day. That sentence is probably the most honest thing I'll think all morning.

### PASSAGE: office_arrival

7:15 AM. The lobby smells like fresh flowers and carpet cleaner and that particular strain of ambition that coats the inside of buildings where people wear lanyards. The security guard nods. I nod back. We've been nodding at each other for three years and I don't know his name. I am a terrible person in exactly the average way.

Elevator to 39. My office is exactly what you'd expect. Not corner. Not window-facing. Not small enough to complain about, not large enough to take pride in. There's a framed photo of Lena and me from our honeymoon in Santorini on the desk. We're standing on a white balcony, blue water behind us, and she's laughing at something I said. I don't remember the joke. I remember the sound, though. The way her laugh used to hit the air like it was breaking something open.

I boot up my laptop. Emails first. Thirty-seven overnight, twelve flagged, three from Greg Tanaka with the passive-aggressive urgency of a man who sends emails at midnight and expects responses by 6 AM. I scan, reply, file. Scan, reply, file. The rhythm of it is almost soothing, the way treading water is almost swimming.

### PASSAGE: harper_intro

"You misspelled 'deliverables' on slide nine."

I look up. Harper Quinn is standing at the edge of my desk with a revised deck in her hand, printed and color-coded because she's the kind of person who prints and color-codes things in an age of digital everything. Twenty-six years old. Sharp enough to cut glass. Dark hair pulled back, minimal makeup, the posture of someone who decided early that competence was the only currency worth earning.

"Did I?"

"D-E-L-I-V-E-R-A-B-L-E-S. You had an 'A' where the second 'E' goes. It's fine. I caught it."

She doesn't make a big deal of it. That's the thing about Harper. She fixes your mistakes and then moves on, doesn't hold them over you, doesn't file them away for leverage. In a building full of people keeping score, she just plays the game.

"Thanks," I say, and I mean it more than the word carries.

She drops the deck on my desk and leans her hip against the partition. "Did you read Crane's latest memo? 'Synergistic value alignment across stakeholder touchpoints.' I counted fourteen buzzwords in three paragraphs. That's almost five per paragraph. A record."

I laugh. Actually laugh. The sound surprises me. It's been buried under so many layers of professional composure that hearing it feels like finding a twenty-dollar bill in an old jacket.

"Martin wrote that himself?"

"Dictated it, probably. While someone polished his cufflinks." She grins, quick and conspiratorial, and then she's gone, clicking down the hall in her sensible flats, already three steps ahead of whatever conversation comes next.

I watch her go. There's something about the way Harper moves through this building. Like she's mapping it. Like she sees the blueprints behind the drywall. I'm not sure if that makes her an ally or something more dangerous.

I go back to my emails. Scan, reply, file. The building hums with Monday.

### PASSAGE: martin_crane

Martin Crane intercepts me in the hallway at 11:40, which is Martin Crane's way of saying he was waiting for me to walk by. He's fifty-five, six-foot-one, built like a man who played football in college and never entirely stopped believing the world owed him for it. Silver hair swept back. Suit that costs more than my monthly car payment. Handshake like he's testing whether your bones are load-bearing.

"Ashford." He grips my hand. I grip back, the way my father taught me: firm, two seconds, eye contact, release. "Henderson account ready for Thursday?"

"Yes, sir. Three-scenario breakdown, full competitive analysis, updated projections."

"Good." He claps my shoulder. The impact is somewhere between affection and establishing dominance, a gesture he probably picked up from his own father, or from a book about leadership with a wolf on the cover. "You've been solid on this one. Keep it tight."

He walks away. Big strides. The hallway parts for him without him noticing because he's never had to notice.

I stand there for a moment. My shoulder warm where his hand was. Martin represents something I'm supposed to want. He's the endgame of the architecture my father built: the commanding voice, the corner office, the certainty. I should admire him. I do, technically, in the way you admire a monument you walk past every day. With distance. With the suspicion that up close it's smaller than it looks.

### PASSAGE: afternoon_grind

The afternoon is a sludge of meetings and spreadsheets and a lunch I eat at my desk. Turkey on wheat, apple, water. The same lunch four days a week because decisions are a finite resource and I spend mine on things that matter less.

At 2 PM I sit in on a strategy review where Greg Tanaka talks for forty minutes about "leveraging adjacencies" and "right-sizing our go-to-market" and I nod at the appropriate intervals and make notes I'll never read and the whole thing feels like standing in a river of words, waist-deep, waiting for one of them to mean something.

Harper catches my eye from across the table during the phrase "circle back on the bandwidth question" and does something subtle with her left eyebrow that makes me cough-laugh into my coffee. Greg glances at me. I fake a cough. Harper's face is perfectly neutral. She's dangerous.

By 5 PM the building is thinning out. People streaming toward elevators with their coats and their bags and their relief. I stay until 6:15 because that's what Daniel Ashford does. He doesn't leave on time. He leaves late enough to be noticed but not so late that anyone worries.

I shut my laptop. Pack my briefcase. Same four pockets, same four objects. Out the door, down the elevator, into the parking garage. The car smells like leather cleaner and the absence of anywhere I actually want to be.

### PASSAGE: dinner_at_home

Lena is in the kitchen when I get home. The apartment smells like garlic and olive oil, which means she reheated the pasta from two nights ago, which means neither of us went grocery shopping, which means we're living on leftovers and the fumes of a routine that lost its engine somewhere around our third anniversary.

"Hey." She's at the counter, scrolling her phone, wineglass half-empty. She's in leggings and an oversized sweater that slips off one shoulder. She looks good. She always looks good. Petite, auburn hair loose, the kind of face that makes you think of French films and rainy afternoons. She married me because I was safe. I know this. She hasn't said it in those words, but I know it the way you know the weather's changing before you see the clouds.

"Hey. Smells good."

"It's the same thing from Tuesday."

"Still smells good."

She half-smiles. Doesn't look up from her phone.

We eat at the kitchen table with the TV on in the living room, volume low, some reality show she pretends to watch and I pretend to ignore. I talk about work. The Henderson account. Greg's meeting. She nods at the right moments, asks the right follow-up questions, her fork moving from plate to mouth in a rhythm that mirrors the conversation: steady, practiced, automatic.

I watch her for a moment when she's not looking. The way her jaw moves when she chews. The tiny crease between her eyebrows that appears when she's scrolling. Four years of marriage and I know her face like a map I've memorized but stopped using. I could draw it from memory. I couldn't tell you what she's feeling right now.

"How was your day?" I ask.

"Fine. Yoga was good. Sarah's pregnant."

"Sarah from your studio?"

"Mmhm."

The conversation dies the way our conversations always die. Not with conflict or silence but with the mutual, silent agreement that we've both said enough to qualify as communication without actually communicating anything. The dinner plates go in the dishwasher. She pours another glass of wine. I loosen my tie. The evening settles around us like dust.

### PASSAGE: living_room_evening

We sit on the couch. Her legs are tucked under her, phone tilted at an angle I can't see. Instagram, probably. Or texting someone. I have my laptop open, pretending to review tomorrow's prep, actually reading the same paragraph for the fourth time because my brain decided to clock out at 7:30 and my body hasn't caught up.

The silence between us has texture. It used to be comfortable, the kind of silence two people share when words aren't necessary. Now it's the other kind. The kind that exists because words have become too much work.

"I'm going to bed," she says at 9:15. She leans over and kisses my cheek. Brief, dry, perfunctory. The kiss of a woman fulfilling a contract.

"I'll be in soon."

She pads down the hallway. I hear the bathroom door close. Water running. The electric toothbrush. Then nothing. Then the bedroom door.

I sit on the couch and wonder when I became the kind of man who lets his wife go to bed alone four nights a week without feeling anything more than a vague, tired guilt. My father would have called it weakness. My father would have been right, probably, though he'd have been right for the wrong reasons.

I close the laptop. Finish Lena's wine. Head down the hall.

The bedroom is dark except for the glow of Lena's phone. She's lying on her side, scrolling. Because I'm afraid of the answer if the answer is no.

### PASSAGE: sex_scene_main

The sigh. She doesn't know she does it. This small exhalation through her nose, barely audible, that happens every time I touch her with intent. It isn't desire. It isn't refusal. It's something in between, something that sounds like a woman bracing herself for a task she doesn't resent but doesn't want, either.

The lights are off. They're always off. I don't remember who started that. It might have been me, early on, self-conscious about my body. Slender in the way that photographs well but looks unremarkable naked. No definition, no breadth, just a functional frame that does its job and nothing more. Or it might have been her, wanting the privacy of darkness to feel whatever she felt without me watching. Either way, the darkness is ours now, mutually owned, and what happens inside it has all the heat of a scheduled appointment.

I move on top of her. She parts her legs. Not wide. Just enough. Her hands settle on my shoulders with the weight of something already familiar, already catalogued, already filed under "Monday, probably, or Thursday."

I'm inside her. She's warm and technically ready and the mechanics are fine, they've always been fine, mechanics have never been the problem. I move. She responds. The sounds are quiet, polite. Almost professional. A small "mm" on my third thrust. A shift of her hips on the fifth that could be adjustment or encouragement or simply repositioning for comfort.

I think about the Henderson account. I think about whether I responded to Greg's third email. I think about the look on Martin Crane's face when he said "keep it tight" and then I stop thinking about Martin Crane because that's not where I want my head right now, and then I realize the fact that my head went there at all is its own kind of answer.

Four minutes. I finish. The orgasm is functional. A release valve, not a crescendo. My body tightens, empties, relaxes. I exhale against her neck and she pats my shoulder blade the way you pat a dog that just brought back a ball.

I roll off.

"Mm," she says. The same sound from before. I can't parse it. Pleasure? Patience? Relief?

She reaches for her phone. The screen blooms to life in the dark room and her face reappears in that blue glow, scrolling, already somewhere else, already past whatever just happened between us.

I stare at the ceiling.

The ceiling has a hairline crack running from the light fixture to the northwest corner. I've been tracking it for two years. It's grown about an inch. Slower than my marriage is cracking, but more honestly.

*I used to make her scream.*

The thought surfaces like a body from deep water, bloated and unwelcome. Year one. That studio apartment with the thin walls and the neighbor who'd bang on the shared surface at 2 AM. Lena on top of me, riding, biting her lip, failing to stay quiet. Her fingernails leaving crescents on my chest. Her head thrown back, throat exposed, the sound she made when she came, this broken, gorgeous, animal sound that I carried in my chest for days afterward like a trophy.

When did she stop? When did I stop trying to make her?

I turn my head. She's smiling at her phone. Not at me. At something on the screen. Whatever it is, it's funnier than I've been in months.

I stare at the ceiling.

The crack is still there.

### PASSAGE: post_choice_bridge

The night settles. The apartment ticks with the sounds buildings make when no one's listening. Refrigerator hum. Heat cycling. The faint murmur of traffic forty feet below.

Lena is asleep now. Her breathing is even, rhythmic, the soundtrack of a thousand identical nights. I should find it comforting. I used to find it comforting. Now it sounds like white noise, and white noise is just silence wearing a disguise.

I get up. Quietly. Pad to the kitchen in bare feet. Pour a finger of whiskey from the bottle on the counter. Not enough to matter. Just enough to feel something other than the flat hum of a day lived exactly the way it was supposed to be lived.

I stand at the kitchen window. The city is out there, lit up, alive with the chaos and ambition and mess of a million people doing things that matter to them. And I'm in here, in my nice apartment, with my nice wife, with my nice career, drinking nice whiskey, and the word "nice" is a prison sentence served in a room with no bars.

My phone is on the counter. I pick it up. No new emails. No messages. No one reaching for me in the digital dark. I am exactly as needed as the day requires and not a degree more.

I finish the whiskey. Rinse the glass. Place it in the dishwasher beside Lena's wineglass.

I go back to bed. Lie down carefully, not touching her, not touching anything. Close my eyes.

Tomorrow will be the same.

I count to eleven.

### PASSAGE: office_day_2

## WRITING STYLE REFERENCE

**Style:** Literary minimalist contemporary. Influenced by Bret Easton Ellis (American Psycho-era interior monologue, the clinical cataloguing of surfaces), Gillian Flynn (dark interiority, the weaponized self-awareness), and Rachel Kushner (institutional observation, the body as political site).

**Voice hallmarks:**
- Short declarative sentences alternating with longer, winding observations
- Repeated structures for rhythmic emphasis ("Same shower. Same shave. Same shirt.")
- Self-aware narration that undercuts its own observations
- Metaphors drawn from domestic and professional life, never nature
- Humor that's dry enough to crack
- Present tense throughout
- Physical detail as emotional shorthand (the crack in the ceiling, the shoe polish, the sigh)
- No sentimentality. Emotion arrives sideways, through objects and silences
- No em dashes. Commas and periods carry the weight.

**Anti-patterns avoided:**
- No flowery descriptions of weather or landscapes
- No rhetorical questions answered by the narrator
- No "It wasn't X, it was Y" constructions
- No summarizing emotional states directly ("I felt sad"). Instead, the feeling is embedded in the physical world.
